## Structure

Start as a **sole proprietorship**. There is no state filing to set one up, and you can form an LLC later if the business grows. The trade-off is that you are personally liable for the business. Insurance (step 7) is how you manage that risk.

An LLC is formed by filing Articles of Organization with the California Secretary of State. The filing fee is $70 (seen November 2026; confirm current fee). California LLCs are more expensive to keep than in most states: every LLC pays an **$800 annual tax to the Franchise Tax Board**, even in a year with no sales, plus $20 every two years for a Statement of Information (seen November 2026; confirm current amounts). See step 2. Ask an accountant whether an LLC is worth it for you.

## Name

- If your business name includes your surname and does not suggest other owners, for example "Jane Smith Ceramics", you do not need to file a name.
- If you use any other name, for example "Smith and Company" or "Blue Door Gifts", you **must file a fictitious business name statement with your county** in the next step.
- An LLC name must be distinguishable from LLC names already on the Secretary of State's records and must end with "LLC", "L.L.C." or "Limited Liability Company". It cannot use words like bank, trust, corporation or insurance.

The Secretary of State does not check your name against county fictitious business names or trademarks, so search those too. A name reservation for an LLC costs $10 for 60 days (seen November 2026; confirm current fee). Filing a name does not give you exclusive rights to it.

## State income tax

**California taxes individual income**, at rates from 1% to 12.3% (2025 rate schedules, seen November 2026). As a sole proprietor you report your business profit on your California Form 540 using federal Schedule C. There is no separate registration with the Franchise Tax Board, but you may need to pay California estimated tax during the year with Form 540-ES. An LLC files Form 568 and pays the $800 annual tax. Ask an accountant how this applies to you.

## Consumer privacy law

The **California Consumer Privacy Act** applies to for-profit businesses with gross annual revenue of $26.625 million or more, or that buy, sell or share the personal information of 100,000 or more California residents or households (seen November 2026 on the California Privacy Protection Agency site; the threshold is adjusted from time to time). Most new small stores are below this. Separately, the California Online Privacy Protection Act requires **any** commercial website that collects personal information from California residents to post a privacy policy, whatever its size. See step 11.
