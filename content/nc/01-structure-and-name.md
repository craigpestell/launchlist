## Structure

Start as a **sole proprietorship**. There is no state filing to set one up, and you can form an LLC later if the business grows. The trade-off is that you are personally liable for the business. Insurance (step 7) is how you manage that risk.

An LLC is formed by filing Articles of Organization with the North Carolina Secretary of State. The filing fee is **$125 (seen November 2026; confirm current fee)**. After that, every LLC files an annual report by April 15 each year, starting the year after it is formed, and the fee is $200 (seen November 2026; confirm current fee). See step 2. If you later change structure, ask the Department of Revenue whether you need a new sales tax registration (step 4).

## Name

- If you trade under just your own legal name, you do not need to file a name.
- If you use any other name, for example "Jane's Gift Shop", you **must file an assumed business name certificate** in step 2. This applies to an LLC trading under a name other than its own, too.
- An LLC name must end with "Limited Liability Company", "L.L.C.", "LLC" or a similar ending allowed by law, and some words, such as "Bank", "Trust", "Wholesale" and "Insurance", need permission or proof before you can use them.

Assumed names do not have to be unique, and filing one does not give you exclusive rights to it. The Secretary of State has a statewide search of assumed names filed since December 1, 2017. For older filings, check with your county register of deeds.

## State income tax

**North Carolina taxes personal income at a flat 3.99% for tax years after 2025 (seen November 2026; confirm current rate)**. Your profit as a sole proprietor or single-member LLC owner goes on your North Carolina return, and there is no separate registration. You must pay estimated tax during the year if you expect to owe $1,000 or more after withholding and credits. We found no city or county income tax in North Carolina. Ask an accountant what applies to you. Federal tax is in step 9.

## Consumer privacy law

North Carolina does not appear to have a broad consumer privacy law like some other states (checked November 2026; confirm with the North Carolina Department of Justice). The **Identity Theft Protection Act applies to every business, including sole proprietors**: if personal information such as card or account numbers is exposed in a security breach, you must notify the people affected and report it to the Attorney General. See the Department of Justice's security breach page and step 11.
